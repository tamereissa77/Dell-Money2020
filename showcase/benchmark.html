Reading the benchmark

The fraud demo runs on IBM's TabFormer, the standard public card-fraud dataset. Public benchmarks are useful for proving that a system works and how fast it runs. They are far weaker at proving how well a model will work on your data — and on this one, the reason is unusually instructive.

What we found in the data

A single column predicts fraud almost perfectly

In the slice of TabFormer that the standard benchmark tests on, every one of the 2,087 fraudulent transactions occurs in the same city. Not most — all of them.

Why this matters more than the score

On this test slice a single line of SQL beats the model. Any headline accuracy figure taken from it is measuring a property of the dataset, not the capability of the method — and it is discoverable by anyone who groups the frauds by city.

The demo's own explainability output said so all along: its top attribution for almost every flagged transaction is merchant city. The model had learned the shortcut and was reporting it honestly.

Where it comes from

The data generator moves fraud between cities

TabFormer is synthetic. Its fraud is generated in campaigns, and each campaign is concentrated in one geography. The benchmark's train/test split happens to place the entire test period inside a single-city campaign.

Fraud that clusters by geography and time is not unrealistic — a breached merchant, a skimming ring or a BIN attack produces exactly that shape. What is unrealistic is that it is total, with no counterexamples and no competing signal.

The honest measurement

Tested where geography varies, the model beats the rule

We re-ran the pipeline against a period where fraud spans 730 cities. Same model, same features, same code — only an honest test period.

Test periodFraud citiesBest single ruleModelVerdict
2019 (standard benchmark) 1 0.9900 0.9578 Rule wins
2015–2016 730 0.7568 0.7800 Model wins

The number worth quoting is 0.78, not 0.96

On the honest split the model reaches F1 0.78 with precision 0.82, against 0.68 for the best single rule — a 14-point precision advantage that a rules engine cannot reach, because the signal is not in any one column.

That is a smaller headline and a far stronger claim. It is the version that survives a data science team checking it.

On your data

What changes, in both directions

Works in your favour

  • Richer features. Device fingerprint, channel velocity, account tenure, merchant risk history. No single column will be a usable proxy.
  • A real graph. TabFormer gives the model card → transaction → merchant and nothing else. Your networks — shared devices, addresses, beneficiaries, mule structures — are genuinely predictive and are exactly what rules cannot express.
  • The graph contribution here is understated. This benchmark is close to the weakest possible showcase for a graph model.

Works against you

  • Late, partial labels. Chargebacks settle over 60–90 days and confirmed fraud undercounts.
  • Harsher imbalance than the 0.122% base rate in this data.
  • Continuous drift. The adversary adapts, so a model trained across a campaign boundary learns the campaign rather than the behaviour — which is precisely what the 2019 result demonstrates.

Expect absolute F1 on production data to sit below 0.78 rather than above it, mostly because the base rate is harsher and the labels are noisier. That is the honest expectation — and it is also the wrong yardstick.

The metric that transfers

Detection at fixed review capacity

Fraud teams do not buy on F1. They buy on the question they actually face: my team can work 500 alerts a day — how much fraud do I catch? Alongside alert-to-fraud ratio and value-weighted recall.

Why this one holds up

The live demo measures exactly that: it takes an incumbent screening engine's queue, re-ranks it, and reports how much of the fraud appears in the top N — the same investigator effort, both orderings.

Because it is a ranking comparison inside the incumbent's own queue, it is unaffected by the dataset artefact above. It is also the one number a bank can validate against its own historical alerts in about a week, without deploying anything.

Ranking comparison — transfers to your data Throughput and latency — hardware fact, transfers Per-decision explainability — architectural, transfers Absolute F1 — dataset-specific, does not transfer

The engineering shown at the booth — streaming ingestion, GPU scoring throughput, per-decision attribution and the audit trail — is independent of all of this. Those are properties of the system. The model quality question is the one that needs your data to answer, and answering it properly is a short proof of concept, not a slide.
